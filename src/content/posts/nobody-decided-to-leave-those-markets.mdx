Ads get pulled. Markets get dropped one deferred ticket at a time, and nobody signs anything.

**Why it matters:** A centralised company builds one default experience. The default is assembled from the biggest markets, because that is where the requirements, the people and the data come from. Everything else becomes an edge case: a local payment method, a second language, a different holiday calendar, one more regulator.

So the small market does not get a smaller version of the experience. It gets the big market's version with the parts it needed cut out.

Then the loop closes. The cheapest markets to serve are the ones most like the template, which are the markets the template came from. The efficiency ranking proves itself, every quarter, forever.

Satisfaction scores split by market put the small ones at the bottom, and culture takes the blame. Those customers are said to score harder. The verbatims say something narrower: the payment method that isn't there, the form that comes in one language only, the refund that takes three weeks longer than it does anywhere else.

**The evidence:** The second loop is the one that makes this permanent. A business case to fix a neglected market is built on that market's current revenue. Current revenue is already the result of the neglect. You are judging the repair with numbers the damage produced.

- **Year one.** The gap is small, the fix is cheap, the case is weak because the market is small.
- **Year three.** The gap is wide, the fix is expensive, the market is smaller. The case is weaker.
- **Year five.** There is nothing to return to. A local competitor took the ground, the one person who understood the programme has left, and the data stopped arriving so long ago that nobody trusts the baseline.

No one approved that outcome. Every individual deferral was reasonable.

**What to do Monday:** Pick the market you have quietly stopped serving and write one sentence about it. *We are leaving X by date Y, and the minimum we keep running is Z.*

The minimum is three things: data still flows, one named person still owns it locally, and the programme is not switched off. If you cannot write that sentence, you are not prioritising. You are withdrawing, and you would rather not say so out loud.

**The bottom line:** Finite resources are real. Thirty markets cannot all be served properly, and pretending otherwise is not strategy either. The choice worth defending is a decision made once, in the open, with a floor under it. What you have instead is an exit assembled from two years of reasonable deferrals, by nobody, on a date no one can name.
