A benefit available to everyone gets claimed by the people it costs you most to serve.

**Why it matters:** Customer experience is cheap on a slide and expensive in the field. "Frictionless returns" is two words in a deck. In the warehouse it is reverse logistics, handling time, markdown on goods that come back unsellable, and margin that leaves. A call centre answering in ten seconds is a headcount model. The bill arrives monthly. The return on it arrives as a higher score in a survey.

Then there is the part nobody prices. Whoever gets the most out of a benefit is usually whoever costs the most to serve. Free returns are the clean example. A shopper who orders four sizes and sends three back takes more value out of that policy in a month than a steady customer takes out in a year, and the same logic runs through answer times, service levels and uncapped rewards.

So the cost of an improvement never spreads evenly across the base. It concentrates in a tail the business case cannot see, because business cases are built on averages.

Give it three years and that tail becomes the base. The customers who value the benefit most stay longest and bring others like them. Meanwhile the margin funding it comes from your best customers. That is a transfer of value between customer groups, carried out in the name of better experience, and nobody writes it down.

Fashion retail has been correcting this in public. Zara started charging for online returns in May 2022, following Next and Uniqlo. Boohoo and New Look were already there. H&M added a £1.99 fee in 2023. M&S and ASOS held out, which is a reminder that this is a choice about who you want and not a law. Discounters and low-cost carriers have run the deliberate version for decades, where minimal service is a design decision, and customers keep coming back because price and availability matter more to them than courtesy.

The detail worth stealing sits in the exception. H&M kept returns free for loyalty members.

**The evidence:** One analysis, three steps, on data you already hold.

- **Margin deciles.** Rank every customer by margin and split them into ten groups.
- **Benefit cost.** Attribute returns processed, contacts handled and rewards redeemed to each customer.
- **The two columns.** Put them side by side and read which decile consumes the benefit and which one funds it.

**What to do Monday:** Before any experience improvement goes on a slide, make it carry two lines. Who specifically will use this, and what does it cost at today's volume times twelve months. If the answer to the first line is "everyone", the idea is not finished yet.

**The bottom line:** Good customer experience is the version the P&L survives. Being better stays the goal. Price it, aim it at someone, and it will survive contact with the field.
